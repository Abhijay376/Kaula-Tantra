Nudity and Shame: Guruji on Why the Goddesses Are Nude

Someone asks Guruji why the temple goddesses are all women and why some are nude. He answers with Prakriti, the ego, and an old story about Vyasa and his son Shuka.

Teaching of Sri Amritananda Natha Saraswati (Guruji), given as a question and answer. Post title: "Guruji about nudity", posted on Guruji's blog by Vira Chandra on 5 November 2016. Guruji retells a story from the Bhagavatam (Vyasa, Shuka and the nymphs) and refers to the Biblical story of Adam and Eve. The original has a photo of a goddess icon (Sarva Unmadini), which is not copied here. Originally published at amritananda-natha-saraswati.blogspot.com.

In short

  • On nudity and shame, Guruji starts with Prakriti. Indians see the life giving energy of Nature as female, so the goddesses in the temple are women.
  • The goddesses stand for the energies of Nature that move a person from human to divine.
  • Their nudity shows a love with no limits, given to all beings whatever their caste, class, race or country.
  • For Guruji, nudity also means dropping the ego: the sense of "I" and "mine" that keeps people apart.
  • In the Bhagavatam story, the nymphs feel shame when the clothed sage Vyasa walks past. When his nude son Shuka passes, they feel nothing, because Shuka is lost in Nature and is no observer.
  • Guruji reads Adam and Eve the same way. Being ashamed of nudity was the original sin. Nudity itself was not.

Guruji's words

Q: Why are there only women Goddesses, and why are some of them nude? Why are they all so similar?

Guruji: We Indians consider the life-giving energy of Nature, Prakṛti, as female. Nature is full of energies: some erotically enticing, some ferocious, some aesthetically peaceful. The Goddesses in this temple represent the harmonious energies of Nature which move a person from being human to being a divine personality.

What is the nature of divine beings?
To express love towards all beings and all life, irrespective of man-made caste, class, race, or country distinctions. Their love knows no bounds. It is all-pervasive.

This unconditional love pervading all of Nature is shown as the nudity of the icons here.

Nudity does not mean removing clothes alone. It also means removing all patterns of thinking that separate one person from another. It means getting rid of ideas like “these are my people” and “this is me” — in other words, the sense of I and mine, called ego. It means expanding one’s love to all people.

It may help to illustrate these ideas with a story from the Bhāgavatam.

Once, some divine nymphs were bathing nude in a lake. The hundred-year-old sage Vyāsa was passing by. They felt a sense of shame and covered their breasts with their hands.

After a little while, his son Śuka, a sixteen-year-old male, was passing by, totally nude and absorbed in Nature. They did not even notice him. They were playing and laughing.

Vyāsa noticed this and came back to them and asked:
“I am a hundred-year-old sage without any sexual passions. I was clothed. When I was passing, you felt shame. How come you did not feel shame when my son, exhibiting his nudity, was passing by? I do not understand this strange behaviour of yours.”

They replied:
“O venerable sage, we know your greatness. But when you were passing by, we felt that there was a human observer. When Śuka was passing, it was like a bird flying in the sky, like tree leaves rustling, like water flowing over our very bodies. We did not feel that there was anyone observing us. When there is no one else observing, and we are all by ourselves, how and why would we feel a sense of shame? Are we ashamed of ourselves?”

Vyāsa realised that he was not fully absorbed in his true nature; he still felt a separation from it.

The whole of Nature — and billions of living forms in it — are all nude. Only humans cover themselves with their shame.

When Adam ate the apple, he felt ashamed of his and Eve’s nudity. That was the original sin: being ashamed of nudity. Nudity itself was not sin. They were nude before eating the apple, and they were not sinful then.

What this means in practice

In Kaula practice the Goddess is Nature herself, and the body is part of Nature. Guruji's answer gives you a simple test for inner worship. Ask whether you are looking at yourself as Shuka would, absorbed and without judgment, or as an observer who keeps some distance.

He also widens the meaning of "nude". You can sit with a yantra or a deity in your ordinary clothes and still practise this teaching by letting go of "my people" and "this is me" for the length of the puja.

Time outdoors helps too. Guruji points out that birds, trees and water are all nude and none of them feel shame. Watching them is a good way to drop some of the self consciousness we carry around.

Wherever Kaula practice honours a living person as the Goddess, it happens only between willing, consenting adults, with respect at every step. On this site such practices are described only at the level of meaning.

Common questions

Why are Hindu goddesses shown nude?

Guruji says the nudity of the temple icons shows the unconditional love that runs through all of Nature. A divine being loves all life, whatever its caste, class, race or country.

Why are the goddesses at Devipuram all female?

He says Indians see the life giving energy of Nature, Prakriti, as female. The goddesses stand for the harmonious energies of Nature that move a person from being human to being divine.

What is the story of Vyasa, Shuka and the nymphs?

Some nymphs were bathing nude. They covered themselves when the old, clothed sage Vyasa passed by, but did not notice his nude son Shuka at all. They told Vyasa that Shuka felt like a bird or the wind, so they felt no one was watching them.

What does nudity mean in spirituality?

For Guruji it means more than taking off clothes. It means dropping the thoughts that separate people, such as "these are my people" and "this is me", and growing your love to include everyone.

Was nudity the original sin?

Guruji says no. Adam and Eve were nude before they ate the apple and were not sinful then. In his reading, the sin was feeling ashamed of their nudity.
